A degree-12 counterexample to Saniga et al.'s Section 4.4 line-subgeometry conjecture

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Can geometry always identify exactly how many quantum measurement constraints must fail when consistent predetermined outcomes are impossible? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer no to a conjecture of Saniga and colleagues: their main example requires twelve failures, but every proposed geometric pattern selects fifteen constraints.

The measurements are products of single-qubit Pauli observables X, Y and Z, with I representing no operation. Each has outcomes +1 and −1. Certain triples commute and multiply to the identity or its negative, requiring their assigned outcomes to have the corresponding product. Such a triple is a measurement context, represented as a line containing three observable-points. Contextuality means no assignment satisfies all lines; the contextuality degree counts the fewest unavoidable failures. The three-qubit geometry has 63 points.

Saniga and colleagues linked optimal failures in special configurations to a classically embedded split Cayley hexagon. This is not an ordinary polygon: it covers all 63 points, with three selected lines through each. Their broader conjecture would require every contextual line family’s degree to equal its number of lines shared with some such hexagon. The authors instead fix an observable, called the pole, and take all lines among the 31 observables commuting with it. This gives 75 lines, including lines not through the pole. It differs from Muller and colleagues’ noncontextual configuration consisting only of lines through the pole.

The starting point is the known degree-three result for the complete two-qubit geometry, with 15 points and 15 lines. For example, XX, ZZ and YY commute but multiply to the negative identity. Assigning +1 everywhere fails exactly three negative lines. For the lower bound, the authors exhibit ten six-line grids: every point occurs twice, but the number of negative lines is odd. Multiplying each grid’s constraints therefore gives a contradiction. Each grid needs a failure; each line belongs to four grids, so two failed lines cannot cover all ten.

For the pole IIZ, each two-qubit point has two copies, obtained by appending I or Z. There are 15 positive pole lines, while each base line has four other lifts retaining its sign. Copying an optimal assignment to both copies and giving the pole +1 yields twelve failures. Conversely, sixteen choices of copies respecting binary addition each contain a complete two-qubit geometry, requiring three failures. Each non-pole line occurs in four choices, forcing twelve distinct failures. The same counting proves that, for at least two qubits, a full commuting configuration has four times the degree of the complete geometry with one fewer qubit. Measurement symmetries called Clifford transformations move any pole to the coordinate pole; accompanying sign corrections preserve failures.

The hexagon obstruction is simpler. Every ambient line meets the 31-point set in one or three points. A hexagon has 63 lines and three incidences at each of those 31 points. If k lines lie entirely inside, then 93 = 3k + (63 − k), so k = 15, not twelve. A smaller counterexample combines all 15 lines through XII with a Mermin–Peres square, overlapping in two of its six lines. This 19-line family has degree one: the square forces one failure, achieved by assigning +1 everywhere. Every hexagon nevertheless shares at least three pole lines.

The evidence has distinct scopes. The exact values and arbitrary-qubit rule have written proofs. The authors report finite calculations checking signs and small configurations, not an extrapolation establishing the general rule. The manuscript describes a supplied Lean proof of a narrower obstruction at IIZ: its degree is positive and at most twelve, while hexagon intersections are at least fifteen. The exact degree, exact general intersection count, transport to every pole and arbitrary-qubit identity are not established by that Lean statement.

Thus the unrestricted equality fails, while earlier special cases remain intact. Holweck and colleagues showed that classical hexagon complements admit satisfying assignments, so intersection size still bounds contextuality degree from above; here it is strictly too large for every embedding. Using the known full three-qubit degree 63, the general rule also gives degree 252 for each four-qubit full commuting configuration—not for the complete four-qubit space. The argument does not settle neighboring skew-hexagon questions or the proposed global four-qubit description. Determining additional hypotheses that restore equality remains a question.
